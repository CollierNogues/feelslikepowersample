From Cave Paintings to Head-Mounted Displays: A Short History of Immersive Art 

The impulse to make aesthetically pleasing immersive environments that simulate realistic imagery and motion is an ancient one. Edward Wachtel has observed that the 17,000-year-old cave paintings at Lascaux and La Mouthe in France were cinematic, rather than still images; when illuminated by flickering torchlight, the painted figures seem to come alive, moving and fading into and out of view depending on the angle of the light and the shape of the cave’s uneven walls. Such a viewing experience is not only cinematic but immersive: the screen wrapped all the way around its original artists and audiences. 
b.	[aside: In recent years, CAVE-style full-room immersive recreations of ancient caves have become prevalent ways for modern visitors who cannot enter closed caves because the artworks are too fragile. The UNESCO World Heritage Site in northestern China, the Mugou grottoes, is one example (Pure Land); the Yangung Grottoes’s intricately carved walls appeared as an exhibition with live performances in Shanghai in 2025 (“Step Inside”).] 

15,000 years later, around 20 B.C.E., Roman artistocracy designed a windowless banquet room with trompe l’oeil walls made to give the impression that the diners were eating al fresco, surrounded by a beautiful summer garden (Villa Livia at Prima Porta, Grau 29-30). Grau points out that the lighting supports the immersive effect, pouring in from an opening just below the ceiling in a way that anticipates the lighting design of 19th-century painted panoramas. 

Grau’s larger point is that today’s virtual reality is a technological extension of these early examples; immersive art has always been about technological innovation. 
e.	[aside: Sometimes those claims of innovation and progress are implicit. The Yongsan History Museum, housed in the former Yongsan railway station in central Seoul, displays an early pair of stereoscopic spectacles under the wall text describing how Yongsan had won out in the early 1880s over two other potential ports for foreign trade.]
